Every morning, millions of Sikhs look for one thing before they start the day: the **Hukamnama from Sri Darbar Sahib**. It appears on gurdwara notice boards, in Punjabi newspapers, on phones and on television. Yet the word *Hukamnama* has more than one meaning, and newcomers are often unsure whether it refers to scripture, to a letter, or to a religious ruling.

This article explains the word, its three main uses, the particular significance of the Hukamnama from Sri Harmandir Sahib, and how devotees engage with it.

## The meaning of the word

**Hukamnama** (ਹੁਕਮਨਾਮਾ) is made of two words that came into Punjabi from Persian:

- **Hukam**, meaning an order, command or decree.
- **Nama**, meaning a letter, document or written statement.

Literally, then, a Hukamnama is "a letter of command". The word has also taken on a deeper spiritual resonance, because **Hukam** is a key concept in Gurbani. Japji Sahib, the opening composition of Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji, teaches that all of creation exists within the Hukam, the divine order, and that the spiritual path lies in living in harmony with it. When Sikhs speak of receiving the Guru's Hukam, they are drawing on this idea.

## Three ways the word is used

### 1. The daily reading from Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji

This is by far the most common meaning today. Sikhs regard Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji as their living, eternal Guru. When the scripture is opened, a shabad (hymn) is read from a page chosen at random. That reading is received as the Guru's guidance, the Guru's Hukam, for the moment or the day.

This reading is also called the **Mukhwak** ("words from the Guru's mouth") or simply the **Vak**. The terms overlap closely; we explain the nuances in [Mukhwak and Hukamnama: Meaning, Difference and Daily Practice](/articles/mukhwak-and-hukamnama).

### 2. Historical letters from the Gurus

In the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, several Gurus sent written letters to sangats in distant towns. These letters, also called Hukamnamas, might bless a congregation, ask it to send offerings or supplies, invite its members to a gathering, or give instructions. Letters attributed to Guru Tegh Bahadur Ji and Guru Gobind Singh Ji survive, as do letters from Mata Sahib Devan and Banda Singh Bahadur.

Some of these documents are preserved by families and gurdwaras as treasured relics, and historians have collected and published them. They are an important source for understanding how the early Sikh community was organised across great distances.

### 3. Edicts from Sri Akal Takht Sahib

**Sri Akal Takht Sahib**, facing Sri Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, is the highest seat of temporal authority in the Sikh tradition. Edicts addressed to the whole Sikh community are issued from it, typically after deliberation by the **Panj Singh Sahiban**, the jathedars of the five Takhts. These edicts are also known as Hukamnamas. They may concern religious discipline, community matters or the status of individuals.

This use of the word has nothing to do with the daily reading. When a news report says "the Akal Takht issued a Hukamnama", it means an edict, not a shabad.

## Telling the meanings apart

| Use | What it is | Who issues it | How often |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily Hukamnama or Mukhwak | A shabad read from Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji | Taken by a granthi at any gurdwara | Every day, often several times |
| Historical Hukamnama | A letter from a Guru or early Sikh leader | The Gurus and their household, early leaders | Historical documents |
| Akal Takht Hukamnama | An edict addressed to the Panth | Sri Akal Takht Sahib | Occasionally |

When someone asks for "today's Hukamnama", they almost always mean the first use, and usually the reading from Sri Darbar Sahib in Amritsar.

## Why the Hukamnama from Sri Darbar Sahib matters

Every gurdwara takes its own Hukamnama. A small gurdwara in a village or suburb will open Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji each morning and read a shabad just as Sri Harmandir Sahib does, and that Hukamnama is fully valid for its sangat.

Yet the Hukamnama from **Sri Darbar Sahib**, the name many Sikhs use for Sri Harmandir Sahib, holds a special place. Sri Harmandir Sahib is where Guru Arjan Dev Ji installed the Adi Granth in 1604, and it has been the spiritual centre of the Sikh world ever since. Its morning Hukamnama is taken after the scripture is carried in procession from Sri Akal Takht Sahib and opened in the sanctum, and is then shared around the world within hours.

For Sikhs far from Amritsar, reading the same Hukamnama as the sangat at Darbar Sahib is a way of being spiritually present there. Families in different countries often read it together over the phone or discuss it in the evening. Our article on the [daily Hukamnama tradition at Harmandir Sahib](/articles/harmandir-sahib-daily-hukamnama-tradition) describes the morning ceremony in detail.

## How the daily Hukamnama is taken

The procedure follows the **Sikh Rehat Maryada**, the code of conduct published by the SGPC. In outline:

1. After Ardas, the reader opens Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji at random.
2. The reading begins with the first shabad at the top of the left-hand page. If that shabad began on the previous page, the reader turns back and reads it from its beginning.
3. The complete shabad is read. If it is part of a Var, the full pauri (stanza) with its accompanying slokas is read.

The shabad's heading tells listeners the raag and the Guru who composed it. The page number, or **Ang**, is noted so that anyone can find it again.

## How devotees engage with the Hukamnama

People relate to the Hukamnama in many ways:

- **Listening at the gurdwara.** The sangat listens attentively as the Hukamnama is read, often following along.
- **Reading at home.** Many people read the day's Hukamnama from Sri Darbar Sahib with a Punjabi explanation or English translation.
- **Reflection.** Some keep a journal of the Hukamnama and what it asked of them that day.
- **Family discussion.** Parents often read it with children and talk about its meaning.
- **Life events.** A Hukamnama is taken at weddings, naming ceremonies, when moving into a new home and before important undertakings.

## Sharing responsibly

Because the Hukamnama is so widely shared, errors and misattributions do circulate. A few habits help:

- **Check the date.** Forwarded images are sometimes days or years old.
- **Share from the source.** The SGPC's official publication is the most reliable version.
- **Do not edit the text.** Retyping Gurbani by hand introduces mistakes. Share the original.
- **Credit translations.** Every translation is an interpretation; say whose it is.

## Summary

A Hukamnama is, literally, a letter of command. Today the word most often means the daily shabad read from Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji as the Guru's guidance, and above all the reading from Sri Darbar Sahib in Amritsar. It can also mean a historical letter from a Guru or an edict from Sri Akal Takht Sahib. Knowing which meaning is in play makes it easier to understand the news, follow a gurdwara programme, and approach the daily Hukamnama with the attention and respect it deserves.
