Searching for "today's Hukamnama from the Golden Temple" will bring up dozens of images, videos and messages. Some are from the official source and some are not. And once you have the right text, you still face a page of Gurmukhi verse with headings, numbers and markers that are not self-explanatory.

This guide is practical. It explains where to find the day's Hukamnama, how to read its structure, how to use translations, and how to take its message into your day.

> [!IMPORTANT] Evergreen guide, not live content
> This article does not contain today's Hukamnama and will not change from day to day. For the daily reading, use the official sources listed on our [Hukamnama Today](/hukamnama-today) page. NanakShahi does not generate, retype or paraphrase the daily Hukamnama.

## Step 1: Find the official Hukamnama

The daily Hukamnama from **Sri Harmandir Sahib** (the Golden Temple) is taken in the early morning after Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji is opened in the sanctum. The **Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC)**, which manages the shrine, then publishes it, usually as an image or PDF containing the Gurmukhi text and a Punjabi explanation. The morning reading is also broadcast live on the SGPC's own channel.

A few points help you get the right text:

- **Prefer the SGPC's publication** or its broadcast over forwarded copies.
- **Check the date** printed on the Hukamnama, which is often given in both Nanakshahi and Gregorian form.
- **Mind the time zone.** The Hukamnama is taken in the early morning Indian Standard Time. For readers in the UK it is usually available before breakfast. In North America it often appears the previous evening by the local clock.
- **Be wary of old images.** Pictures of past Hukamnamas are frequently re-shared as if they were new.

## Step 2: Understand the heading

Every shabad in Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji begins with a heading. It tells you three or four useful things.

### The raag

Most of Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji is arranged by **raag**, the musical measure in which a hymn is to be sung. You will see names such as Sorath, Dhanasri, Bilawal or Asa. The raag carries a mood and is part of the meaning; kirtan at Sri Harmandir Sahib follows these raags. Our article on [live kirtan from the Golden Temple](/articles/live-kirtan-golden-temple) explains more.

### The Mahalla

**Mahalla** (often shortened to M, as in M5) identifies the Guru who composed the shabad. All the Gurus whose compositions appear in the scripture signed their verses with the name "Nanak", so the Mahalla number tells you which Guru is speaking:

| Heading | Composer |
| --- | --- |
| Mahalla 1 | Guru Nanak Dev Ji |
| Mahalla 2 | Guru Angad Dev Ji |
| Mahalla 3 | Guru Amar Das Ji |
| Mahalla 4 | Guru Ram Das Ji |
| Mahalla 5 | Guru Arjan Dev Ji |
| Mahalla 9 | Guru Tegh Bahadur Ji |

Some Hukamnamas come from compositions of the **Bhagats**, saints such as Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas and Farid, whose hymns are included in the scripture. In that case the heading names the Bhagat.

### Ghar

You may also see **Ghar** followed by a number. It is a musical instruction, generally understood to indicate the rhythm or musical setting. Readers who are not singing can simply note it.

### The Ang

The **Ang** is the page number in the standard 1,430-page edition of Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji. It lets you find the shabad again, read what comes before and after it, and compare translations. Most Hukamnama publications print the Ang at the top or bottom.

## Step 3: Find the Rahao

Many shabads contain a line or couplet marked **Rahao** (ਰਹਾਉ), meaning "pause". It is widely understood as the central theme of the shabad, the idea around which the other verses turn.

A helpful way to begin is:

1. Read the Rahao line first.
2. Ask what it is saying, in the simplest terms you can.
3. Read the other verses, one by one, and notice how each relates to the Rahao.

This keeps you from getting lost in individual images and helps you see the shabad as a whole.

## Step 4: Read the verses and their numbering

Verses in Gurbani end with numerals inside double vertical bars, such as ॥੧॥ and ॥੨॥. These mark the end of each stanza. At the end of the shabad you may see several numbers together, indicating its position within a larger set of hymns.

If the Hukamnama is from a **Var**, a composition of stanzas (pauris) with accompanying couplets (slokas), it will include one or more slokas followed by a pauri.

## Step 5: Use the Punjabi explanation and translations

The SGPC's publication normally includes a **Punjabi explanation** (*viakhya*) beneath the Gurmukhi. If you read Punjabi, start there.

For English readers, several translations of the complete scripture are widely used, including the translation by **Bhai Manmohan Singh**, published by the SGPC, and that of **Dr Sant Singh Khalsa**, which is available on many websites and apps. Punjabi readers also rely on commentaries such as Professor Sahib Singh's *Guru Granth Darpan*.

Keep three things in mind:

- **Every translation is an interpretation.** Gurbani is poetry, rich with imagery and layered meaning. A single English sentence cannot capture all of it.
- **Compare where you can.** Reading two translations side by side often reveals meaning that one alone misses.
- **Return to the original.** Even basic familiarity with key Gurmukhi words, such as *naam*, *hukam*, *haumai*, *gurmukh* and *manmukh*, makes translations far more useful.

> [!TIP] Learning a little Gurmukhi
> The Gurmukhi script has 35 core letters plus vowel signs, and many adults learn to read it within a few weeks of regular practice. Being able to follow the original text, even slowly, changes how the Hukamnama feels.

## Step 6: Reflect and apply

The Hukamnama is understood as guidance, so the final step is personal. Some ways people do this:

- **Write one line.** Note the Rahao, or one verse that stood out, and carry it through the day.
- **Ask one question.** "What is this asking of me today?"
- **Read it aloud with family.** Discussing it at breakfast or in the evening is a long-standing habit in many homes.
- **Listen to katha.** Short explanations of the day's Hukamnama are given at many gurdwaras.

## Step 7: Share it with care

If you share the Hukamnama, share the official image or a link to it, not a retyped version. Include the date. If you add a translation, credit the translator. Avoid adding your own words in a way that could be mistaken for part of the Hukamnama.

## Evergreen knowledge versus daily content

It helps to be clear about what can and cannot be written in advance:

- **Evergreen:** how the Hukamnama is taken, how to read a shabad's heading, what Rahao means, how translations work. That is what this guide covers.
- **Daily:** the specific shabad read at Sri Harmandir Sahib today. That can only come from the source, each day.

Any website or message that claims to give you "today's Hukamnama" should be able to show where it came from. If it cannot, treat it with caution.

## Summary

To read today's Hukamnama well: get it from the official source, note the raag, Mahalla and Ang, start from the Rahao, read each verse in its light, use translations thoughtfully, and take one idea into your day. The steps are simple. What they open up is a daily conversation with Gurbani that many Sikhs find is the most important part of their morning.
