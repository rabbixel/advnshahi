Stand outside a gurdwara early in the morning and you may hear people ask, "What was today's Vak?" Someone else might say Mukhwak, and a notice board may carry the heading Hukamnama. All three words point to the same moment: the reading of a shabad from Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji, received as the Guru's guidance.

The words overlap, but they are not identical. This guide explains each term, how the reading is taken, and the many places the practice appears in Sikh life.

## The vocabulary

### Mukhwak

**Mukhwak** (ਮੁਖਵਾਕ) joins *mukh*, "mouth", and *vak*, "utterance" or "saying". It means "words from the mouth", understood as words spoken by the Guru. The term puts the emphasis on the source: this is the Guru speaking. Gurdwara publications, including those from Sri Harmandir Sahib, often use it as a heading for the day's reading.

### Vak

**Vak** (ਵਾਕ), often spelled *vaak*, simply means "saying" or "utterance". To take a reading is to **lena vak**, "take a Vak". It is the everyday, informal word, and you will hear it most in conversation.

### Hukam and Hukamnama

**Hukam** means command or order and is a central idea in Gurbani, where it refers to the divine order within which all creation exists. **Hukamnama** means "letter of command". When used for the daily reading, it emphasises that the shabad is to be received as the Guru's instruction.

Unlike Mukhwak and Vak, Hukamnama has other meanings too: historical letters from the Gurus to distant sangats, and edicts issued from Sri Akal Takht Sahib. Our explainer [Hukamnama Explained](/articles/hukamnama-sri-darbar-sahib-explained) covers these.

## So what is the difference?

In practice, the three words are used interchangeably for the daily reading. The differences are ones of emphasis:

| Term | Literal meaning | Emphasis | Other meanings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mukhwak | Words from the (Guru's) mouth | The Guru as speaker | None |
| Vak | Saying, utterance | Everyday, informal | None in this context |
| Hukamnama | Letter of command | Guidance to be followed | Historical letters; Akal Takht edicts |

If you are writing or speaking about the daily reading, any of the three is correct. If you are writing about an Akal Takht ruling, only Hukamnama applies.

## How a Vak is taken

The procedure is set out in the **Sikh Rehat Maryada**, the Sikh code of conduct published by the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee. The steps are simple and deliberate:

1. **Ardas first.** The reading follows Ardas, the Sikh prayer, which ends with a request for the Guru's guidance.
2. **Open at random.** The reader opens Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji without choosing a page.
3. **Begin at the top of the left-hand page.** The Vak is the first shabad starting at the top of the left-hand page.
4. **Go back if necessary.** If that shabad began on the previous page, the reader turns back and reads it from its beginning.
5. **Complete the shabad.** The whole shabad is read. If it is part of a **Var**, the full pauri with the slokas that precede it is read.

The page number (Ang) is announced or noted, so the sangat can find the shabad again.

> [!NOTE] Who may read
> The Sikh Rehat Maryada states that any Sikh, man or woman, may take a Vak. What matters is the ability to read Gurbani correctly and the respect shown to Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji.

## The daily rhythm at a gurdwara

A Vak is taken at several points in the day:

- **At the morning Prakash**, when Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji is ceremonially opened. This is the reading most people mean by "today's Hukamnama".
- **At the end of a diwan**, after the concluding Ardas, so that each gathering closes with the Guru's word.
- **At other points in the day**, according to each gurdwara's custom, including in the evening.

The morning reading at **Sri Harmandir Sahib** in Amritsar has special significance and is shared worldwide. Our article on the [daily Hukamnama tradition at Harmandir Sahib](/articles/harmandir-sahib-daily-hukamnama-tradition) describes how it unfolds.

## "Mukhwak Darbar Sahib today"

People searching for the Mukhwak from Darbar Sahib are looking for the shabad read that morning at Sri Harmandir Sahib. It is the same reading as "today's Hukamnama from the Golden Temple". The SGPC publishes it each day with a Punjabi explanation and broadcasts the morning reading.

Our [Hukamnama Today](/hukamnama-today) page lists the official sources. NanakShahi does not reproduce the daily text unless it comes directly from a verified source. ## The Vak in family life

The practice of taking a Vak reaches far beyond the daily programme.

### Naming a child

At a Sikh naming ceremony, the family gathers before Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji, Ardas is offered and a Vak is taken. The first letter of the first word of the shabad is announced, and the family chooses a name beginning with that letter. **Kaur** is added to a girl's name and **Singh** to a boy's.

### Weddings

The **Anand Karaj**, the Sikh marriage ceremony, is solemnised in the presence of Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji and includes Ardas and a Hukamnama.

### New beginnings

Many families hold a short programme with Ardas and a Vak when moving into a new home, starting a business, or before a journey or important decision. The intention is to begin with the Guru's guidance.

### Remembrance

At the conclusion of the rites after a death, the programme also includes Ardas and a Hukamnama.

## At home

Families who keep a saroop (copy) of Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji at home maintain it with the same respect as a gurdwara: in a clean, dedicated place, opened and closed each day with the proper procedure. Such households take their own daily Vak.

Many other families read the day's Hukamnama from Sri Darbar Sahib instead. Apps that display a "random shabad" are popular for study and reflection, but many Sikhs draw a clear distinction between these and a Vak taken from Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji itself. If in doubt about the right practice, ask at your gurdwara.

## Etiquette when the Hukamnama is read

If you are present when the Vak is read in a gurdwara:

- Keep your head covered and sit quietly, facing Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji.
- Stop conversations and put your phone away.
- Listen attentively. Many people follow along in a gutka or on a screen.
- Remain seated until the reading and the announcement of the Ang are complete.

## Why the practice matters

Taking a Vak expresses a core Sikh conviction: that Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji is the living Guru, and that the Guru speaks to the sangat through Gurbani. Opening the scripture at random is not about chance or fortune-telling. It is an act of humility, a willingness to receive whatever guidance the Guru gives, whether comforting or challenging.

That is why many Sikhs return to the Hukamnama again and again through the day. The shabad read at dawn becomes a lens for everything that follows.

## Summary

Mukhwak, Vak and Hukamnama all name the shabad read from Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji as the Guru's guidance. Mukhwak stresses that these are the Guru's words, Vak is the everyday term, and Hukamnama stresses the command, while also having other historical and institutional meanings. The practice follows a simple procedure set out in the Sikh Rehat Maryada and runs through Sikh life, from the morning Prakash to naming a newborn child.
